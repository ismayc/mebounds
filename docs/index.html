The Mebound

mebound /ˈmiː·baʊnd/ · noun. A rebound a player grabs off her own missed shot. Coined for Angel Reese, who missed, followed, and finished so often that the stat needed a name.

"Mebound" started as a dig. It began as internet shorthand for the accusation that Angel Reese pads her rebounding numbers by missing layups and grabbing them back. She reclaimed the word, and the argument has now run for three seasons, mostly on vibes and clip selection. But underneath the noise sits a genuinely testable claim, which is rarer than it sounds in a sports argument: how often does she actually rebound her own miss, is it a lot compared to everyone else, and does it come at the cost of the rest of her rebounding?

So we tested it. We replayed every regular-season possession of her career, the 2024, 2025, and 2026 WNBA seasons, alongside the three NBA seasons played over the same years: 4,417 games, just over two million play-by-play events, 445,738 rebounds. Each rebound was flagged as a mebound when the board and the immediately preceding missed shot belonged to the same player, with identical rules applied to both leagues, and every player's totals were reconciled against ESPN's box scores before a word of this page was written.

Correction, October 19, 2026. A check against the NBA's own play-by-play found that ESPN files each season's All-Star games and the in-season cup finals as regular-season games, so the July 28 version of this page counted 17 exhibition games (4,417 regular-season games, not 4,434). Games played also counted did-not-play rows. Every number, chart, and table on this page now uses the corrected data. Reese's career line moves from 136 mebounds on 1,136 rebounds (12.0%) to 132 on 1,111 (11.9%); her rookie season is 446 rebounds in 34 games, not 457 in 40. She still leads the WNBA in mebound count every season, but no longer in mebound share every season: in 2025 Elizabeth Williams (25 of 223 rebounds, 11.2%) edges her 11.1%, and both feeds agree on both players. Rechecking every claim against the data also removed two that did not hold even on July 28: she ranks second in total rebounds each season rather than leading the league, and her 2026 lead in mebound count was never her largest relative to games played. The NBA leaders barely move (Jokić 94 in 2023–24, Antetokounmpo 54 in 2024–25, Towns 50 in 2025–26). The July 28 page is in the repository's history.

The short version: the nickname is measurably earned. Just not in the way the dig intended. Reese recovers her own misses at three to four times the league rate while carrying a rebounding leader's workload, and the NBA comparison below shows how rare that combination is on either side of basketball.

Her rebounds, unpacked

One rebound in eight is her own miss coming back

Split Reese's rebounds into three piles: defensive boards, offensive boards off teammates' misses, and mebounds. The signature pile is remarkably steady: 13.0% of everything she grabbed as a rookie, 11.1% in 2025, 11.1% so far in Atlanta. Put differently: she has recovered 18–23% of her own missed shots each season. The league as a whole recovers about 5%.

The shape of the three piles tells its own story. Her rookie season produced 446 boards in 34 games, 13.1 a game, the most per game in the WNBA. A third of her offensive rebounds that year were her own misses coming back. The lower 2026 share of her offensive boards (25.4%) is less a fading habit than a changed diet: in Atlanta she is collecting more offensive boards off teammates' misses (94 already, versus 81 in all of 2025) while still converting a league-best 18% of her own misses into second chances. And one detail the play-by-play makes plain: all 132 career mebounds came off missed field goals. She has never once rebounded her own missed free throw.

The WNBA field

Nobody in her league mebounds like her

Reese has led the WNBA in mebounds in all three of her seasons, and the margins are not close. As a rookie she out-mebounded the runner-up, Aliyah Boston, 58 to 29. That is a two-to-one gap. In 2025 the runner-up was her own teammate: Chicago put three players in the league's top six (Reese, Kamilla Cardoso, Elizabeth Williams), a reminder that mebounds are partly a team-context stat. An offense that misses a lot leaves a lot to recover. Through the 2026 All-Star break the count is 32 to 18 over a three-way tie of Breanna Stewart, Kamilla Cardoso, and Azura Stevens.

Rate tells nearly the same story as volume. Across the 58 player-seasons with 200+ rebounds in these three years, the highest mebound share of total rebounds is hers in 2024 and 2026. In 2025 her Chicago teammate Elizabeth Williams edged her, 11.2% to 11.1%, on 25 mebounds and 223 rebounds. By count the trademark holds every season; by rate, two seasons of three.

The toggle below re-scores the same five players by what share of their offensive rebounds are their own misses, and it adds a nuance worth pausing on. In 2025 Reese tops that view too (34.1%), and in 2024 only A'ja Wilson's 35.4% edges her 33.7%. But flip it for 2026 and the picture inverts: Azura Stevens converts 60% of her offensive boards from her own misses, on barely half of Reese's mebound volume. Reese's share sits at a career-low 25.4%. The two measures reward different things, and Reese is the only WNBA player in the count top five all three seasons.

The NBA over the same years

The NBA has volume kings and chase artists. Reese is both

Plot every NBA player-season against two questions: how much do you rebound? and how much of it is your own miss? Two archetypes appear. Niko​la Jokić owns the volume corner: most mebounds in the league three years running, but they're only ~9% of his enormous haul. Zion Williamson, Devin Booker, and Jaylen Brown own the rate corner: 12–13% of their rebounds are self-service, at half the volume. Overlay Reese's three seasons and she sits where nobody in the NBA does: a rebounding leader's workload with a chase artist's rate. Switch the vertical axis to offensive rebounds only and the guard archetype sharpens. Over half of Brown's and Booker's offensive boards are their own misses. Reese's 25–34% sits above the league averages (20.6% NBA, 23.3% WNBA) without being the extreme. Her separation is the combination, not either number alone.

Read the corners of the default view. The bottom-right is where traditional centers live: enormous rebound totals, tiny self-service share, because their offensive boards are mostly cleanups of teammates' misses. The upper-left belongs to shot creators: modest totals, but when they do hit the offensive glass it is usually to chase their own long miss. The gray cloud hugs the 5.2% league line; almost nobody escapes it upward past 10%. Of the 365 player-seasons that cleared 300 rebounds over these three years, only 18 cross that line.

Two ways to mebound

Guards chase their own misses. Bigs inhale everyone's.

Ask what fraction of a player's offensive rebounds are their own misses and the spectrum splits by role. Shot-creating guards live at the top. When Skylar Diggins or Jamal Murray gets an offensive board, it's almost always their own long miss. Paint anchors like Rudy Gobert live at the bottom: endless offensive boards, almost none their own. Reese sits a third of the way up, which is unusual at her volume. Jokić is the NBA's oddity: a center who mebounds like a guard.

The mechanism is shot diet. A missed three rebounds long, often straight back toward the shooter. That is why Diggins, Murray, Harden, Irving, and Dončić convert 59–75% of their rare offensive boards from their own misses. A missed layup rebounds short into a crowd of centers. That is why Gobert's share sits near 8% despite almost 300 offensive boards a season. Reese and Jokić occupy the interesting middle: interior players who miss short, stay with the play, and finish the recovery themselves. Jokić's 40.7% career own-miss share is, for a center, the strangest number in this data.

The NBA's mebound king

In the NBA, the crown belongs to Jokić

By raw count the NBA's mebound race has one winner: Jokić led all three seasons: 94, then 84, then 73, for 251 in total. Alperen Şengün is the only repeat runner-up (147 across the last two seasons alone). The names below them rotate almost completely year to year, which says something: sustained mebounding at volume is rare. Even so, Reese's rookie share (13.0%) tops every NBA player-season in the sample except Zion Williamson's 13.05% in 2023–24. And Zion did it on half her per-game rebounding.

Flip the toggle to % of own offensive rebounds and the crown still doesn't move: among each season's five count leaders, Jokić also posts the best own-miss share: 42.2%, then 42.0%, then 37.8%. He is the volume king and, among high-volume bigs, the most self-sufficient offensive rebounder at once. The toggle is mostly useful for what it reveals about the runners-up: pure-volume centers like Capela (17.9%), Zubac (16.7%), and Clingan (11.8%) collapse in that view because their offensive boards are overwhelmingly teammates' misses. Aaron Gordon, Giannis, and Scottie Barnes hold shares in the thirties. Two crowns, one head, three years running.

The verdict

A dig, reclaimed and measured

The word was coined as criticism: the implication that a rebound of your own miss is a stat-padder's rebound. The data reads the other way. League-wide, only about one rebound in twenty is a mebound; converting your own misses at 18–23% while also ranking among the league's top two rebounders every season means doing two different hard things at once. A mebound is a possession saved by the same player who risked it. It takes effort, positioning, and a shot diet that keeps the miss recoverable.

So the measurable claim inside the nickname is not that Angel Reese's rebounds are cheap. It is that nobody in either league combines her rebounding workload with her rate of self-recovery. Not the NBA's volume king. Not its chase artists. On that claim, three seasons and two million play-by-play events are unambiguous.

Method

Definition. A mebound is an offensive rebound credited to the same player who missed the immediately preceding shot attempt (field goal or free throw) in the same period. The lookback skips administrative events (substitutions, timeouts, replay reviews); a jumpball or any other live event between miss and board breaks the link.

Data. ESPN play-by-play via the sportsdataverse dumps (the data behind wehoop and hoopR), identical schema for both leagues. WNBA seasons 2024, 2025, and 2026 (through games of July 25, 2026) and the NBA seasons ending in the same calendar years. Regular season only, minus the 17 All-Star and cup-final games ESPN files as regular-season games. Per-player play-by-play rebound totals reconcile against ESPN's box scores within 1 rebound league-wide per WNBA season and ~0.02% for NBA seasons. All of Reese's 132 mebounds came off her own missed field goals, none off missed free throws.

Related denominators. This page mostly uses mebounds ÷ total rebounds (and ÷ offensive rebounds). Social posts often quote mebounds ÷ own missed shots. By that lens Reese recovered 22.7%, 23.2%, and 18.1% of her own missed field goals by season against a ~5% WNBA average. All three framings are computed from the same events and agree in direction; they differ only in denominator.
